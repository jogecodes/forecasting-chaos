01 / CHAOS AND PREDICTABILITY

Why can a simple system
be hard to predict?

Chaos is, in its simplest form, what we call it when a system follows fixed rules and still behaves in ways that are hard to foresee. Nothing in it is random: start from exactly the same state and you get exactly the same trajectory. Yet two almost identical starting states can drift apart until their trajectories look nothing alike. This is called sensitivity to initial conditions.

The real issue? No starting state is ever known exactly. Measurements have limited precision and calculations introduce small errors, which chaotic dynamics amplify until the forecast no longer matches reality. Short-term predictions of a chaotic system can still work well, but in the long run, they are doomed to stray.

02 / Lorenz and the butterfly effect

What happens when things
start out a bit differently?

Edward Lorenz was a mathematician trying to understand why weather is so hard to forecast. In his 1963 study, he distilled air convection down to just three coupled equations:

dxdt=σ(y−x)\frac{dx}{dt}=\sigma(y-x)dydt=x(ρ−z)−y\frac{dy}{dt}=x(\rho-z)-ydzdt=xy−βz\frac{dz}{dt}=xy-\beta z

Three numbers (x, y, z) pin down the state of the system as a point in the “phase space”, and the equations move that point step by step along a trajectory. The rules are fixed, so the same starting point always gives the same path. But a tiny change in the starting point can leave you somewhere completely different after the same amount of time. Lorenz found this out by accident: he restarted a simulation from rounded numbers, and the new run soon looked nothing like the original.

Draw every point a trajectory visits and a shape emerges: the Lorenz attractor, the butterfly-shaped curve we introduced at the top of the page. The path never repeats itself, yet it never leaves this shape. It circles one wing of the butterfly a few times, then jumps to the other, and keeps switching in an order that never settles into a pattern. In Lorenz's air convection model, each wing is a direction of circulation of the air, clockwise or anticlockwise, so every jump is the flow reversing.

There are three things worth noticing about these trajectories:

01

They’re deterministic

Both runs follow the same three equations. Given exactly the same starting state, they would trace the same path. Even the apparently erratic switches between the wings come from those equations; no random decision is being made along the way.

02

They’re sensitive

The initial difference can be too small to distinguish on the plot, but it still changes the state from which the next step is calculated. As the system evolves, that difference can grow until the two paths stop lining up. That’s why the precision of a starting measurement matters so much to a forecast.

03

Yet they’re still structured

Even after the two paths separate, they keep tracing the same butterfly-shaped structure. It becomes hard to say where either one will be at a particular time, but the region they explore remains recognizable. Losing a precise trajectory forecast doesn’t mean losing every useful description of the system.

The forecasting problem

What makes a chaotic system
hard to forecast?

At every step, a recursive model feeds its prediction back into itself. A small error changes the next input. The next prediction compounds that error. Sensitive dynamics make the consequences grow.

There are two problems to separate: uncertainty about the starting state, and error in the learned dynamics. Even an analytical model faces the first. A learned model adds the second.

03 / The forecast laboratory

How long does a prediction stay useful?

Spoiler: not much. A forecast can follow the system closely at first, but will then drift away as small errors get amplified by chaotic behavior. Here, four models learn from simulated trajectories and try to predict what happens next. Replay their predictions alongside the original simulation to see how long they stay close and what happens after they separate.

The forecasting models

The systems above provide the data. The four models below learn from those trajectories, then try to continue them on their own. For each system, fitting uses 32 training trajectories and checkpoint selection uses four separate validation trajectories. After the observed history ends, each model feeds its predictions back into itself.

Polynomial dynamics

Fits polynomial expressions for how the state changes, then integrates those learned equations forward. This gives it a structure particularly well suited to the low-order equations used here.

Transformer

Uses attention to read the previous 24 states and predict the next change. It’s a numerical sequence model trained on simulated trajectories.

GRU

A recurrent neural network with a gated memory. It reads the same 24-state history, carrying information forward through that sequence to predict the next change.

Residual MLP

A feed-forward neural network that predicts the next change from the latest complete state. Because the full state is observed here, it has the information needed to describe the next step without a longer history.

Each run uses the same equations but starts from a different state. Switching runs changes the example the models have to predict. All four forecasters use the same history within a run.

What do these scores mean? +

RMSE measures the typical size of a coordinate’s prediction error, with larger misses contributing more strongly. The last column lists every physical coordinate; each error is in that coordinate’s own units.

NRMSE first divides each physical coordinate’s error by its standard deviation in the training data, then averages squared errors over coordinates and forecast steps and takes the square root. “Early” uses the first 180 forecast steps; “Full” uses the complete saved horizon. Neural scores evaluate the ensemble mean.

These are scores for the selected trajectory. Overlapping forecast windows and repeated inspection limit generalization claims. The systems have different native time scales, so these values are intended to compare forecasters within the same system and example.

Long-horizon point errors often become large once paths separate. They measure exact tracking, while the state-space view can reveal whether a model still produces broadly recognizable motion. The retrospective episode diagnostic answers a separate, development-calibrated question about sustained mismatch and recovery.

How to interpret the comparison +

Each system uses 32 training trajectories and four validation initial conditions. The original evaluation has four test trajectories with two overlapping forecast origins each, plus one reference origin. Here we show the reference and the first origin of each test IC, selected without ranking their results. Test views have been inspected during development; this is an exploratory evaluation, not a fresh blind test.

Neural models have seeds 7, 11, and 23, trained for 1,600 updates per fit. Checkpoints were selected using separate 180-step validation rollouts. All models receive physical state; Van der Pol models also receive known forcing phase, advanced exactly. Neural models predict increments. Polynomial degree 2 or 3 is selected on validation and integrated with RK4.

The error chart averages squared coordinate errors after dividing each coordinate by its training standard deviation, then takes the square root. It is distinct from the retrospective breakdown rule, which also checks slope and curvature. Seed trajectories illustrate fitting variability; they are not calibrated uncertainty bands. Native simulation time units differ across systems.

The shared episode rule uses validation memory τ, confirmation window 5τ, at least 80% bad samples, and passing gaps no longer than τ. The multiplier was calibrated on four models of one inspected Van der Pol trajectory. Lorenz and Rössler have no human visual validation yet. Withholding the GRU label changes the selected multiplier and flags an earlier episode: the rule is sensitive.

02 / STRUCTURE
The right prior is powerful.
Learned polynomial dynamics fits polynomial expressions for how the state changes, then integrates them forward. That is a particularly good match for these systems: Lorenz and Rössler have quadratic interactions, while forced Van der Pol adds a cubic term and a known forcing phase. The model gets a useful head start from choosing the right kind of expression.
In Run 1 of each system in section 03, the polynomial model had the lowest early normalized RMSE of the four forecasters:
Lorenz: 0.000067 over the first 1.8 time units, compared with 0.0069 for the best neural model. The retrospective rule marked sustained mismatch at 14.00 units, versus 5.96–7.29 for the neural models.
Rössler: 0.000026 over the first 9 time units, compared with 0.0066 for the best neural model. Sustained mismatch was marked at 99.05 units, versus 21.15–50.00 for the neural models.
Forced Van der Pol: 0.172 over the first 9 time units, compared with 0.332 for the best neural model. Here the sustained-mismatch markers were much closer: 11.10 units for polynomial dynamics and 10.80–11.05 for the neural models.
That early advantage did not mean winning every measure. Over the complete saved horizons, the neural models had lower normalized point errors on Lorenz and Van der Pol. These are inspected examples under the same equations and parameters; the time units differ between systems, and the onset markers depend on the diagnostic rule. A well-matched prior helped, but these results do not establish a universal ranking of learning methods.

      
03 / PHYSICS
Reconstructing a past is different from forecasting a future.
A corrected physics-informed neural network reconstructed withheld observations inside its fitted interval accurately, yet extrapolated poorly beyond it. In the reference run, position RMSE was about 0.0012 for reconstruction and 2.91 for extrapolation. The intervals and tasks differ; these values illustrate the gap rather than a common benchmark score.

      
04 / MEASUREMENT
“When does it fail?” is a research question too.
Two curves can meet at the same value while heading in different directions. To check whether a forecast is still following the motion, the diagnostic compares every physical variable in three ways: its value, its slope (the first derivative) and its curvature (the second derivative). Matching the position alone is not enough if the way it is changing has already drifted away.
Slopes and curvatures are estimated by fitting a small quadratic curve to the latest five samples of each trajectory, using only its current and past values. Each kind of error is normalized by its own variation in the training data and smoothed over a short window. A sample counts as a mismatch if any variable’s value, slope or curvature exceeds the tolerance.
One bad sample does not establish a breaking point. At least 80% of a complete confirmation window must show mismatch, without an extended return to agreement. The window is five times a memory scale estimated from how quickly the validation trajectories lose correlation. Brief passing gaps are tolerated; a longer return to agreement counts as recovery. The solid line marks the retrospectively identified onset, and the dashed line marks when enough later evidence had accumulated to confirm it.
This gives a working definition of sustained breakdown, rather than a claim that a forecast can never look close again. The rule is still provisional: its confirmation multiplier was calibrated on four model examples from one Van der Pol trajectory, and Lorenz and Rössler have not yet had independent visual validation. Changing the settings can move the marker.
